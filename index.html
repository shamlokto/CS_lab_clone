How bacteria build their cell envelope

Streptococcus pneumoniae hides inside a sugar coat, the capsule. We work out how the cell makes it, how it fits that coat around a dividing cell, and which genes it cannot live without, to find new ways to prevent and treat pneumococcal disease.

Why it matters

A sugar coat that decides who lives

Pneumococcus is a leading cause of pneumonia, child mortality and years of life lost worldwide, killing roughly 700,000 people each year. To establish an infection it builds a capsular polysaccharide (CPS) that shields it from the immune system.

Because the capsule is the outermost layer of the cell, it sets how the bacterium looks to the host, and every clinically used pneumococcal vaccine targets it. We combine genetics, systems biology, biochemistry and cell biology to learn how the capsule is made and controlled in space and time, and how that knowledge can be used for glycoengineering, vaccines and drugs.

Featured · Science 2025

Mapping genetic interactions across a whole genome

Dual transposon sequencing builds pools of double mutants and reads them out with paired barcodes. In S. pneumoniae it sampled 73% of the 1.3 million possible double gene deletions, and turned up new players in pathways long thought to be understood.
